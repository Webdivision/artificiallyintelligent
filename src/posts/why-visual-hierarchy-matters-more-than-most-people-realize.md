Sometimes the best design lessons come from really simple examples.

I recently came across a graphic shared by [Kyle Sorvick](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kylesorvick/) that made the point immediately. It showed how the eye naturally moves through information when a layout is built the right way. No confusion. No second guessing. You look at it, and the design tells you exactly where to go first.

**That is hierarchy.**

I think hierarchy is one of the most overlooked parts of design, and honestly, one of the biggest reasons communication breaks down.

When hierarchy is working, people do not have to stop and figure out what they are supposed to look at first. The design handles that for them. Their eye goes to the main message, then the supporting message, then the smaller details. It feels easy because it is supposed to.

When hierarchy is missing, everything starts fighting for attention.

That is where a lot of design starts to fall apart. People think the issue is the color palette, or the font choice, or the layout in general. Sometimes that is true. But a lot of the time, the real problem is that nothing has been prioritized clearly.

If everything is bold, nothing stands out.

If everything is large, nothing feels important.

If every part of the message is trying to be the headline, people tune out.

I see this all the time, not just in branding, but in websites, ads, presentations, emails, signage, packaging, really everywhere. When hierarchy is clear, the message feels easier to take in. When it is not, the whole thing starts to feel noisy, even if the design looks decent on the surface.

And that noise costs attention.

Most people are not studying a design. They are scanning it. They are making a quick decision about whether it feels clear, confusing, worth reading, or easy to ignore. Hierarchy is a big part of what shapes that reaction.

That is also why strong design often looks simpler than it really is.

A clean piece of design can feel obvious when you are looking at the finished result, but there is usually a lot of thinking behind that clarity. Type size, spacing, contrast, placement, and pacing all play a role. None of it should be random. Good designers are guiding the viewer through the message, whether the viewer realizes it or not.

That is why I think hierarchy matters so much.

It is not just a technical design principle. It is one of the clearest ways to control communication and make sure your message actually lands.

So the next time something feels off in a design, it may be worth stepping back and asking a different question. Not “Should I change the font?” or “Do I need more color?” but:

**Is the hierarchy clear?**

Because if it is not, the rest may not save it.
